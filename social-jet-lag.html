Why you can't sleep
on Sunday night

Social jet lag affects 70% of adults — and most have never heard of it. Sleeping in at weekends shifts your body clock forward, making Sunday nights feel like you're trying to sleep in a different time zone.

The Sleep SOP 08/2026 3 min read 4 peer-reviewed sources
TL;DR — The short version
  • Social jet lag is the mismatch between your internal body clock and your social schedule — it affects most working adults every single week
  • Sleeping in on weekends shifts your body clock forward — making Sunday nights feel like trying to sleep at midnight in a different time zone
  • Even 1 hour of social jet lag is associated with a 33% higher risk of obesity, higher rates of depression, and worse metabolic health
  • Around 70% of adults experience some level of social jet lag — it's not rare
  • The fix is simpler than it sounds: narrowing the gap between your weekday and weekend wake times to under 1 hour makes a significant difference

Why Sunday nights feel like insomnia

It's 11pm on Sunday. You need to be up at 7am. You're not remotely sleepy. By the time you finally drop off it's midnight or later, and the alarm feels brutal. Monday morning arrives and you're already exhausted before the week has begun.

This isn't random bad luck or stress about the week ahead. It's almost certainly social jet lag — and it has a clear, biological explanation.

Your body runs on a roughly 24-hour internal clock called the circadian rhythm. This clock controls not just when you feel sleepy but when your body temperature drops, when cortisol rises, when melatonin is released, and dozens of other physiological processes. It is anchored primarily by light — specifically, morning light — and by the timing of your daily behaviours.

When you sleep in on Saturday and Sunday — even by just an hour or two — you shift that clock forward. Your brain receives the message that morning is later than it actually is. So on Sunday night, when your clock says "it's only 10pm equivalent", your body isn't ready to sleep at 11pm. You've given yourself the biological equivalent of flying west for the weekend and then trying to go to bed on local time.

How common is it and how bad is it?

Social jet lag was first formally described and named by chronobiologist Till Roenneberg at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich — the same researcher whose work underpins tip card 01 on this site. His analysis of sleep timing data from tens of thousands of participants found that around 70% of the adult population experiences some degree of social jet lag, with an average discrepancy of about 1 hour between their biological sleep timing and their social schedule.

The health consequences of even that modest 1-hour misalignment are substantial. A 2012 study in Current Biology found that each hour of social jet lag was associated with a 33% higher risk of being overweight or obese. Separate research has linked social jet lag to higher rates of depression, worse metabolic markers, higher rates of smoking and caffeine use, and lower academic and work performance. These effects appear to be driven by the same mechanisms as shift work — chronic circadian disruption disrupts the timing of hormone release, metabolism, and immune function in ways that accumulate over time.

Are you a night owl or just sleep-deprived?

Chronotype — your natural tendency to be a morning person or an evening person — is largely genetically determined. Genuine night owls have a delayed circadian phase: their body clocks naturally run later, and they would fall asleep at 1am and wake at 9am if left to their own devices. This is not laziness. It's biology.

The problem is that society is structured around early morning schedules — school start times, work hours, social conventions — that are incompatible with late chronotypes. Genuine night owls face a structural version of social jet lag every weekday, forced to wake several hours earlier than their body clock demands. Research by Roenneberg and others has shown this is one reason night owls have consistently worse health outcomes on average — not because their chronotype is unhealthy, but because the world asks them to violate it every day.

The distinction between "night owl" and "socially jet-lagged" matters practically. If you're sleeping in on weekends because you genuinely need to catch up on lost sleep, the root problem is insufficient sleep during the week — fix the sleep debt at source. If you're staying up late and sleeping in purely for social reasons, narrowing that gap is the most important thing you can do.

How to fix it — without giving up your weekends

The goal is not a perfect schedule. You don't need to wake at exactly the same time every single day of the year. The research suggests that keeping the gap between your earliest and latest wake time to under 60–90 minutes is where most of the benefit lies.

Prioritise the wake time, not the bedtime. As with all sleep science, the most powerful anchor is when you wake up, not when you go to bed. Set a fixed wake time for weekends that is no more than 1 hour later than your weekday time — and get up at that time even if you went to bed late.

Get morning light immediately after waking. Morning light is the most powerful signal your circadian clock receives. Getting outside within 30–60 minutes of waking — or sitting by a bright window — helps anchor your clock to the actual local time rather than your shifted internal time.

On Sunday evenings, move your bedtime slightly earlier. If you've slept in on Saturday and Sunday, you can start nudging your clock back by going to bed 15–30 minutes earlier than you normally would on Sunday night. Don't force sleep — but create the conditions for it earlier.

Avoid bright light late on Sunday night. Bright screens and artificial lighting in the hours before bed delay melatonin release and push your clock later. On Sunday evenings in particular, dimming lights earlier than usual helps signal to your body that night is coming.

Bottom line

Social jet lag is the most common sleep problem most people have never heard of — and it's affecting about 70% of adults every week. The Sunday night insomnia you experience, the groggy Mondays, the mid-week energy dip — these are not just "how weekdays feel". They are often the measurable consequence of shifting your body clock by 1–2 hours every weekend and then trying to snap it back. The fix doesn't require giving up late nights — it just requires keeping the gap between your earliest and latest wake time under an hour or so.
